Methods

Participant Recruitment

We aim for a ground-truth that represents the average perspective of a population. We thus used our pilot studies to estimate the number of ratings needed to approximately capture the US population mean. We recruited a sample of respondents stratified by the most common Ethnicities in the US according to the 2024 US Census1, and additionally by political leaning (i.e. conservative, moderate, liberal) for speeches. We recruit participants from Prolific2, and online participant recruitment platform. Participants were stratified based on information they complete when signing up as a respondent, according to categories determined by Prolific: White, Black/African American, Hispanic/Latino, and three Asian sub-categories, East Asian, South Asian, South East Asian, which we combined into a single ‘Asian’ category. For speeches, we also recruited participants stratified by political spectrum (Conservative, Moderate, Liberal). We estimated the target number of ratings separately per text type (lyrics / speeches), as we expected that each form of text varies in its degree of ambiguity. We recruited a total 300 participants: 100 each stratified by Ethnicity completed the lyrics and the first speeches pilot, and 100 participants stratified by political orientation completed the second speeches pilot.

We conducted pilot studies with the primary aim of estimating the number of ratings needed per text type (lyrics vs. speeches), following a procedure similar to DeBruine & Jones (2018). In our case, this procedure involved bootstrapping estimates of inter-rater reliability on data comprised of a) a relatively large number of ratings on, b) a relatively small sub-sample of the items to be rated. In each pilot study, 100 participants rated 14 items (either lyric or political speech excerpts). We bootstrapped estimates of inter-rater reliability by sub-sampling numbers of raters, and then estimating a) the Pearson correlation between the sub-sample mean, and the overall sample mean, and b) inter-rater reliability (DeBruine & Jones, 2018). We sub-sampled in increments of 5, ranging from 5 to 50, with replacement, 50 times for each increment: i.e. we sampled 5 raters 50 times, 10 raters 50 times, 15 raters 50 times etc. up to 50 raters 50 times, all with replacement. As we aim to represent the mean of a population of raters, we estimate inter-rater reliability as ‘general consensus’ and not ‘absolute agreement’ via intraclass correlation coefficient (type ICC2k), using a value of 0.75 as threshold to indicate sufficent, or “good”, agreement (Koo & Li, 2016). Thus for a given number of raters, we estimate a sampling distribution representing their degree of general consensus, and whether the the mean consensus exceeds the 0.75 threshold. Although we did not see a recommended threshold for pearson correlations in (DeBruine & Jones, 2018), we set a threshold of 0.9 to indicate sufficient, or “high” correlation between sub-sample and overall sample means.

Survey

The primary instrument in all surveys is the 10-item Short Schwartz Value Survey (SSVS) questionnaire developed by (Lindeman & Verkasalo, 2005), which participants completed on the Qualtrics survey platform. Each of the 10 questionnaire items in the SSVS is a specific value along with descriptive words e.g. “POWER (social power, authority, wealth, influence on people and events)”. We asked participants to read the instructions from the Schwartz Value Survey3 in order to introduce the concept of personal values, and to instruct them on how to complete the questionnaire (Schwartz, 2005). Participants then completed the questionnaire about themselves. The response format is a 9-point likert scale which ranges from -1 (Opposed to their values) and 0 (Not at all Important), to 6 (Very Important) and 7 (Of Supreme Importance) (Lindeman & Verkasalo, 2005).

Participants completed an adapted version of the SSVS for each of the lyric or speech excerpts they rated. We used an SSVS version similar to (Demetriou et al., 2024), in which the instructions are slightly adapted for annotation. Firstly, we ask participants to respond to the questions while considering the Speaker, which we distinguish from the Author of the text i.e. the person or people responsible for selection of words and their structure. In the case of Speeches, the “Speaker” was defined as the president delivering the speech, as opposed to e.g. staff who may have written it all or in part, or other individuals who occasionally appear in the speech transcript. In the case of Lyrics we explain that the Author of the lyrics may write from the perspective of someone or something else, whom we call the “Speaker”. We instruct participants to respond with the Speaker of the lyrics in mind. Secondly, we present participants with a ‘Not Applicable’ response option, as we expect that excerpts of text give an incomplete picture of the values of the Speaker. Thus, we give participants the option to indicate “NA” to indicate “Not Applicable” if they feel no information on the value is present in the text. We thus gather 10 likert-scale responses per item (lyric / speech excerpt) being rated, within a range of -1 to 7, and with possible NAs.

A secondary instruction in our pilot included a comprehension quiz, which asked three questions shown in random order, each of which included a brief scenario: (1) “If you feel a SPEAKER seeks POWER above all else as a life-guiding principle, how would you respond?”, to which the correct response was at the high end of the scale (i.e. 5, 6, or 7), (2) “If you feel a SPEAKER doesn’t give any indication of how important POWER is to them in the lyrics, how would you respond?” to which a correct response was “NA”, and (3) “If you feel a SPEAKER avoids POWER more than anything else, or sees it as completely unimportant to them, how would you respond?” to which a correct response was on the lower end of the scale (i.e. -1, 0, or 1). Questions were accompanied by the single item, POWER. After each question, the survey displayed the correct response, along with a brief explanation e.g. “If you feel a SPEAKER seeks POWER above all else as a life-guiding principle, respond with a ‘7’, indicating it is of Supreme Importance to them”. Participants most often mistook “NA” and low-end scale responses in Quiz items 2 and 3. Thus, for Quiz 2 where the correct response was “NA”, 13% of participants indicated “0” and “9”% indicated “-1”, where 56% correctly indicated “NA”. Similarly in Quiz 3, 13% of participants incorrectly indicated “NA” where 50% and 20% of participants correctly indicated “-1”’ and “0” respectively. Thus, in the minds of many annotators, “NA” and the lower-end of the scale bear similar meaning.

We collected a number of exploratory measures which we felt might affect participant ratings, but for which we had no specific prediction. For lyrics we asked participants “Do you think the SPEAKER and the AUTHOR are the same person?”, and “Do you recognize the song that the lyrics come from?”, and for speeches we asked participants “Do you recognize the president that delivered the speech?”, and “Which party do you think the president belongs to?”. Following the SSVS for each lyric or speech excerpt, we also assessed participant confidence in their ratings by asking “How confident are you in your ratings of these lyrics? / this speech?”, to which participants could respond on a scale from 1 (Completely Confident) to 5 (Completely Unconfident).

Our survey also checked if participants were paying attention. As per Prolific guidelines, we include an attention check at the halfway point of the annotation component of the survey. The check appeared to be another song lyric, with the lyrics being instructions to participants to indicate “7” for the value of Stimulation, “0” for all other values. Participants who responded with “-1” or “NA” instead were also considered passing if they were consistent in the ratings of all values other than Stimulation. Participants who failed the attention check were returned to Prolific, and automatically replaced.

The procedure used was reviewed by the Independent Review Board at Delft University of Technology [# etc.]. For complete survey materials, see Supplementary Materials:Survey.

Lyrics data

We used the same initial 2200 pool as in Demetriou et al. (2024), who attempted to gather a stratified sample of lyrics. An ideal scenario would have been to have a target number of songs, randomly sampled from within clearly defined strata (groves2009survey?). They acknowledge that 1) the population of songs is enormous, with many streaming services reporting over 100M songs 4, and that no target sample size could be calculated, and 2) some strata affecting the use of language in song lyrics are less clear, or ‘fuzzier’, than others: e.g., the year of release, which may reflect different events or time-specific colloquial slang, is relatively more clear than e.g. popularity for music, although it can be estimated from various sources such as hit charts, which is more clear than genre, for which there may be some overlap in human labels, but no clear musicological taxonomy (Liem2012MusicGap?). Thus, the target number of sampled songs was determined by budgetary limitations, and the sampled strata were acknowledged to be ‘fuzzy’: (1) year, the release date according to Musixmatch meta-data; (2) popularity, operationalized as the frequency with which an artist appeared on a playlist frequency in the MPD[DBLP:conf/recsys/ChenLSZ18]; (3) genre, estimated from topic modeling on Million Song Dataset artist tags (schindler2012facilitating?); (4) lyric topic, through a bag-of-words representation of the lyrics data. Genre and lyric topic were treated as categorical variables, and popularity and year were divided into equally spaced bins, with year divided into decades (60s, 70s, 80s, and so on). When sampling, Demetriou et al. (2024) attempted to get as close as possible to even numbers of tracks within these 4 dimensional cells for their intiial pool of 2200 songs.

Demetriou et al. (2024) sampled their initial pool of 2200 songsfrom the Million Playlist Dataset (MPD)5. The first 30% of each song comprised the lyric excerpts in the initial pool, and was provided by Musixmatch6, a lyrics and music language crowdsourcing platform whose members contribute, verify, sync, and translate lyrical content. Although crowdsourced lyrics may deviate from the officially authored lyrics, Musixmatch engages in steps to verify quality of content, including spam detection, formatting, spelling and translation checking, and manual verification by community curators, and a local team of Musixmatch editors. Demetriou et al. (2024) manually screened songs to confirm that the lyrics matched the metadata of the song, selecting a total of 380 songs. Screening was conducted independently initially, and disagreements were resolved via discussion. Screening primarily confirmed that the song metadata matched the song title and artist title if searched on the internet. Screening secondarily removed songs that were (1) not in English; (2) completely onomatopoetic or nonsense; (3) comprised of single words or single phrases; (4) consisted of too few words. Authors of this work further screened songs until a total of 1200 potential songs were considered usable. The first 800 were reserved for the main study, and the last 14 were used for the pilot study. We used 14 songs from the end of the list for our pilot study, and a total of 800 songs from the beginning of the list for annotation.

Speeches data

Speeches were sampled from U.S. Presidents delivering State of the Union Addresses, from the UVA Miller center repository 7. Speech excerpts were stratified within (1) party, (2) president, (3) speech.

Pilot Study

Determining annotator sample strata

We estimate ICC2k overall, and for subgroups separately. For our lyrics pilot, we observe overall ‘good’ ICC2k estimates, with the exception of SELF-DIRECTION, which shows a large confidence interval that exceeds our threshold. These ICC2k scores overall do not appear to improve when we estimate them separately for each Ethnicity.

This is not the case for our two pilots which used political speech data. Overall, agreement on the speech data was unusably poor, however it appeared to improve when estimated separately for each ethnicity, with most values showing ICC2k scores over the threshold. Note that estimation for HEDONISM is generally poor in subsamples, and that STIMULATION is poor in some subsamples.

We observe similar results for our second speeches pilot, which show substantially higher ICC2k scores when they are estimated separately for each political spectrum category.

Conclusion

We tentatively conclude that Ethnicity will have a substantially smaller effect on ratings of song lyrics than either Ethnicity or Political Spectrum will have on ratings of speeches. Thus, the sample for our main study data collection will be stratified by Ethnicity, and for political speeches, it will also be stratified by political affiliation.

Estimating the number of raters

The results from our bootstrapping procedure show adequate ICC2k scores with 15 ratings for lyrics, in both ICC2k scores, and correlations between subsample means and the overall mean.

For both samples in our speech pilots, we observe a bimodal distribution in ICC2k scores, with peaks nearing our threshold, but also around 0.

Our sample of annotators in our first speeches pilot were stratified by Ethncitiy. Our bootstrapping analysis shows sufficiently high correlations between subsample means and the overall mean at around 40 ratings, however, ICC2k scores do not reach sufficiently high ICC2k scores even with 50 ratings.

Our sample of annotators in our first speeches pilot were stratified by political affiliation. We observe a similar pattern, where the correlations between subsample means and the overall mean reach our threshold 35 ratings, but ICC2k scores are not sufficiently high even with 50 ratings.

Conclusion

We thus proceed with a target median 15 ratings per song lyric excerpt. Based on bootstrapped correlations between subsamples and the overall sample, as well as budgetary constraints, we aim for median 40 ratings per political speech excerpt.

Inter-rater reliability

In contrast to our pilot study, in general we observe sufficient ICC2k scores, with no visible improvement when estimated by ethnicity or political leaning.

ICC2k scores for lyrics ratings appear to be exceptionally high overall, and when estimated separately within Ethnic groups, and do not seem to improve when estimated separately for each Ethnic group.

Ratings of political speeches exceed our threshold overall, though ratings do not always meet the threshold when estimated separately within Ethnic groups. In general, the estimates are visibly worse than for lyrics. Once again, we see issues with HEDONISM being poorly estimated for one group, and STIMULATION is poorly estimated for another group.

We observe a similar pattern when separating groups by political affiliation.

MDS plots of text annotations

When examining the MDS plots for our lyrics annotations, we generally see acceptable patterns with most values appearing next to appropriate neighbors, although STIMULATION and HEDONISM appear transposed, and ACHEIVEMENT shows a shift from the perimeter of the circumplex. These patterns are visibly worse for speeches, where we observe a strong shift for HEDONISM from the perimeter of the circumplex.

Model approach selection

We then determine a general modelling approach in order to estimate the effect of Ethnicity on ratings of lyrics, and Ethnicity and Political spectrum on ratings of political speeches.

Conclusion

Model fit was consistently worse for ordinal mixed effects models, vs. general or generalized linear mixed effects models. We thus proceed to our specification curve analysis using general mixed effects models for ease of interpretation and computation.
