\subsection{General Methods}\label{general-methods}

Across three pilot studies and four waves of our main study, we gathered
data to estimate how people perceived the values present in two forms of
text: song lyrics and political speeches. We conducted pilot studies
with the primary aim of estimating the number of observations for each
piece of text, per text type (\texttt{lyrics} vs.~\texttt{speeches}). We
conducted our main study to gather perceptions of values in text more
broadly, one for lyric and one for speech excerpts. We stratified our
participant samples by self-reported ethnicity for both lyrics and
speeches (\texttt{Asian}, \texttt{African\ American\ /\ Black},
`\texttt{Latino/Histpanic}, \texttt{White}), and for speeches we further
stratify participants by self-reported political leaning
(\texttt{Conservative}, \texttt{Moderate}, \texttt{Liberal}). We used an
existing sample of lyrics roughly stratified across four strata
(\texttt{year} of release, \texttt{genre}, \texttt{popularity}, and
lyrical \texttt{topic}). We sampled political speeches of U.S.
presidents, stratified by \texttt{president} and by \texttt{speech}. As
studies generally shared the same overall procedure to recruit
participants and gather observations, we herein describe the stimuli,
survey design, and participant recruitment procedure.

Our procedures were reviewed, determined to have minimal risk to
participants, and thus approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee
at Delft University of Technology\footnote{Letters of approval \#: 4011,
  4024, 4152, \& 4507}. The study plans and analysis were preregistered
on the Open Science Framework (\url{https://osf.io/fhq75}).

All analyses were conducted in \texttt{R} (R Core Team, 2025) within the
\texttt{RStudio} integrated development environment (RStudio Team,
2025), along with the \texttt{here} (Müller, 2020) helper functions for
managing file paths. Data wrangling and statistical analysis relied on
the \texttt{tidyverse} suite (Wickham et al., 2019), helper functions
from \texttt{rlang} (Henry \& Wickham, 2024) and \texttt{ggplot2} for
visualization (Wickham, 2016). Reliability analyses were conducted with
the \texttt{psych} package (Revelle, 2023), mixed-effects models were
estimated with \texttt{lme4} (Bates et al., 2015), and p values from
statistical tests were estimated with \texttt{lmerTest} (Kuznetsova et
al., 2017). Additional APA formatting and reporting used \texttt{gt}
(Iannone et al., 2023) and \texttt{papaja} (Aust \& Barth, 2022).

\subsubsection{Stimuli}\label{stimuli}

We aimed to gather two sets of stimuli, one each representing diverse
lyrics, and political speeches. Both sets were developed using
stratified sampling approaches. See Appendix A: Stimuli for further
details regarding stimuli data and sampling procedures.

\paragraph{Song Lyrics}\label{song-lyrics}

We used the same initial pool of song lyric excerpts as Demetriou et al.
(2024), who obtained lyrics with permission via the API of
Musixmatch\footnote{https://www.musixmatch.com/}. Musixmatch
crowdsources lyrics by hosting a platform where users add, correct,
sync, and translate the lyrics to songs. Musixmath further engages in
quality assurance which includes spam detection, formatting, checking
spelling and translation, and manual verification, via their employee
editors and a community of curators that function similarly to
moderators on forums like Reddit.

Demetriou et al. (2024) aimed at gathering a diverse sample of songs
using a procedure they called ``fuzzy stratified sampling''. An ideal
scenario would have been to gather a representative sample of lyrics,
with a pre-determined target number of songs, randomly sampled from
within clearly defined strata (Groves et al., 2009). However, Demetriou
et al. (2024) note that the population of songs is enormous, with many
streaming services reporting over 100 million songs in their
catalogues\footnote{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparison\_of\_music\_streaming\_services},
and that no work to their (or our) knowledge has been conducted to
determine a complete list of strata, or an appropriate target number of
songs on which to gather perceptions of values. Further, while some
strata that may relate to language use in song lyrics are conceptually
clear, such as the year of release which may indicate time-appropriate
slang or current events, other strata are far less clear: e.g.~(1) the
year of release is a conceptually clear stratum compared to (2)
popularity for which no single ideal method for its estimation exists
despite a plurality of approaches, which in turn is more clear than (3)
genre for which there may be some overlap in human labels, but no
universally agreed upon musicological taxonomy (Liem et al., 2012),
which in turn is practically more clear than (4) the topic of the
lyrics, for which topic estimation is likely to be imprecise. Demetriou
et al. (2024) thus aim for a diverse rather than a fully representative
sample, by taking a stratified sampling approach while acknowledging
that the strata are ``fuzzy'', and some important strata may be missing.

Briefly, Demetriou et al. (2024) uniformally randomly sampled an initial
pool of 60,000 artists in the Spotify Million Playlist Dataset
(MPD)\footnote{https://research.atspotify.com/2020/09/the-million-playlist-dataset-remastered/}
(Chen et al., 2018). The MPD contains the metadata of approximately 2
million individual tracks from approximately 300,000 artists, as they
were arranged in playlists by users. Demetriou et al. (2024) then drew
the metadata of every song from the entire discography of those 60,000
artists from the Spotify API and checked the availability of those songs
in the Musixmatch database, resulting in large pool of approximately
510,000 songs from approximately 15,400 artists. Musixmatch provided the
first 30\% of the lyrics of each of the 510,000 songs via their API.

From the pool of available 510,000 songs, Demetriou et al. (2024) then
drew a sample of 2,200 song lyrics, the number of which was determined
by their budgetary limitations. Songs were sampled within four ``fuzzy''
music strata: (1) \texttt{year}, the release date according to
Musixmatch meta-data, which might contain some errors; (2)
\texttt{popularity}, operationalized as the frequency that an artist
appeared on a playlist in the MPD (Chen et al., 2018); (3)
\texttt{genre}, estimated from topic modeling user-generated tags of
artists in the MPD (Schindler et al., 2012), which were drawn from
Last.fm and merged into the MPD\footnote{http://millionsongdataset.com/};
and (4) \texttt{lyric\ topic}, via topic modelling on a bag-of-words
representation of the lyrics data. They observed a skew such that more
recent and popular artists and songs appeared substantially more on
playlists, and were far more likely to be sampled. Popularity had an
especially severe skew. To increase sample diversity, popularity was
divided into 7 ordinal bins which were approximately equal in terms of
the number of songs in each bin. Year of release was divided into
equally spaced ordinal bins with year being divided into decades (60s,
70s, 80s etc.), and genre and lyric topics were treated as non-ordinal
categorical bins.

Demetriou et al. (2024) then manually screened songs for inclusion in
the study. Screening primarily confirmed that the song metadata matched
the song title and artist name if searched on the internet, and that the
lyrics in the sample matched the lyrics of the song when listening to a
streamed version. Screening secondarily removed songs that were (1) were
more than approximately 20\% non-English words or characters; (2)
completely onomatopoetic or nonsense; (3) comprised of repeated single
words or single phrases; or (4) consisted of too few words. Demetriou et
al. (2024) selected a total of 380 songs. We (authors of present work)
further screened songs using the same requirements until a total of
1,200 songs were considered usable. The first 800 excerpts were used in
the main study, and the next 14 were used for the lyrics pilot study. As
some songs lyrics contained non-lyric words, indicating e.g.~section
(i.e.~verse or chorus), artist, or time stamp, included songs were
manually corrected to only contain the song lyrics. Songs most
frequently removed were completely non-English or contained a large
number of non-English words, e.g.~reggae songs written in Jamaican
Patois or k-pop / j-pop which contained words in English as well as
Korean or Japanese, followed by songs that had very few or single
repeated words such as dance and electronic music.

\paragraph{Political Speeches}\label{political-speeches}

We chose the U.S. State of the Union addresses as the overall corpus, as
the addresses are a long-running, formally recorded corpus of public
speech, that has regularly been delivered to congress each year, by
presidents of both parties of a two-party system. Speeches were sampled
from the UVA Miller center repository\footnote{https://millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches},
which maintains an archive of presidential communications, including
presidential speech recordings and transcripts. Presidents generally
gave a single State of the Union address each year of their presidency.
Our samples were therefore stratified within (1) president, and (2)
speech. We excluded all speeches before 1900 so as to sample language
that is approximately current, and the single speech by Zachary Taylor
so as not to oversample a single speech. Speeches were grouped by
president. Within each president, every speech was divided into a
proportional number of chunks, with presidents who gave fewer speeches
contributing more chunks per speech and those with many speeches
contributing fewer: e.g.~7 per speech from 2 term presidents, and 14 per
speech from single term presidents. Each president contributed roughly
the same number of chunks (M\textsubscript{chunks} = 55.3). From each
chunk, we then sampled from the 200th word to the end of the sentence it
was in. 328 excerpts were then randomly selected, 44.8\% of which were
from Democratic presidents (M\textsubscript{excerpts} = 16) vs.~55.2\%
Republican presidents (M\textsubscript{excerpts} = 15). The first 300
were used in the main study, and 14 each were used in two pilot studies.

\subsubsection{Participants}\label{participants}

We aimed to gather responses that approximate the average perspective of
a target population, and possibly sub-groups of that population, of the
values present in text. We chose the United States as the target
population, given that lyrics are in almost all the most popular songs
in the US\footnote{https://faroutmagazine.co.uk/how-many-number-one-instrumental/?utm\_source=chatgpt.com},
and that the US presidents are likely familiar to US residents, as are
the language and topics discussed. Across all studies, participants were
recruited using online survey participant recruitment platform
Prolific\footnote{prolific.com}, to draw a sample that was stratified
based on information participant self-reported demographics at
registration, according to categories determined by Prolific. We
exclusively drew participants who reported residing in the United
States, and that were fluent in English. To see if perspectives between
sub-groups of the population varied, our sample of respondents was
stratified by the 2024 US Census\footnote{https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/US}
most common ethnicities: (\texttt{Asian},
\texttt{African\ American\ /\ Black}, `\texttt{Latino/Histpanic},
\texttt{White}). The options Prolific provided for specific ethnicity
that were closest to the ethnicities listed in the US Censues were:
White, Black/African American, Hispanic/Latino, and three Asian
sub-categories, East Asian, South Asian, South East Asian, which we
combined into a single `Asian' category. We then aimed to recruit
approximately equal numbers in each category. For perspectives on
\texttt{speech} excerpts, we additionally stratified our samples by
political leaning: (\texttt{Conservative}, \texttt{Moderate},
\texttt{Liberal}).

The procedure, essentially a set of survey components, in order, is
shown in Figure 1. In order to ensure that participants understood the
task, the survey (1) asked participants to complete a questionnaire
about themselves, (2) gave a 3-item comprehension quiz that gave
feedback on the correctness of the response after each item, (3)
repeated the instructions multiple times, and (4) contained an attention
check to filter out low effort responses. For complete survey materials,
see see Appendix B: Survey.

\paragraph{Adapted Short Schwartz Value
Survey}\label{adapted-short-schwartz-value-survey}

The primary measurement instrument in all surveys is the 10-item Short
Schwartz Value Survey (SSVS) questionnaire developed by (Lindeman \&
Verkasalo, 2005), which participants completed on the
Qualtrics\footnote{Qualtrics.com} survey platform. Each of the 10
questionnaire items in the SSVS is the name of a specific value in the
Schwartz value inventory, along with a few descriptive words
e.g.~``POWER (social power, authority, wealth, influence on people and
events)''.

Participants first completed the questionnaire about themselves to
familiarize themselves with the task and with the Schwartz taxonomy of
values. We asked participants to read the instructions from the Schwartz
Value Survey\footnote{Retrieved from
  https://scholarworks.gvsu.edu/orpc/vol2/iss2/9/ March, 2024} in order
to introduce the concept of personal values, and to instruct them on how
to complete the questionnaire (Schwartz, 2005). The instructions explain
that participants are to rate the following values as their importance
as ``a guiding principle in my life'' using the given response format.
The original, unbalanced likert scale in the Schwartz Value Survey
(Schwartz, 2005) is also used in the Short Schwartz Value Survey
(Lindeman \& Verkasalo, 2005): a 9-point Likert scale which ranges from
-1 (Opposed to their values) and 0 (Not at all Important), to 6 (Very
Important) and 7 (Of Supreme Importance). Values are ``ordered by
relative importance'', essentially as a list of values ranked by
priority Schwartz (1992). The SVS instructions therefore ask
participants to first indicate the values that are at the very top
i.e.~7 (Of Supreme Importance) or the very bottom -1 (Opposed to their
values) of their personal value list using the extreme points of the
likert scale. The instructions also explain that there normally are only
one or two of these extreme values. Participants are then instructed to
fill in the likert scales for the remaining values.

\paragraph{General Instructions}\label{general-instructions}

After completing the SSVS about themselves, participants then completed
an adapted version of the SSVS for each of the lyric or speech excerpts
they rated, thus producing annotations representing their perception of
the values expressed by the text. Participants were shown a set of
instructions for their primary task, the rating of values for each text
excerpt. We used an SSVS version similar to (Demetriou et al., 2024), in
which the instructions are slightly adapted. Firstly, for
\texttt{lyrics} we asked participants to respond to the questions while
considering the \emph{Speaker} i.e.~the person or thing whose
perspective the lyrics reflect, which we distinguish from the
\emph{Author} of the text i.e.~the person or people responsible for
selection of words and their structure. In the case of
\texttt{speeches}, we defined \emph{Speaker} as the president delivering
the speech, as opposed to the \emph{Author} who may be the president,
but may also be a team of speech writers and/or other staff, or other
individuals who occasionally appear in the speech transcript. Secondly,
we present participants with a `Not Applicable' response option, as we
expect that excerpts of text give an incomplete picture of the values of
the Speaker. Thus, we give participants the option to indicate
\texttt{NA} to indicate ``Not Applicable'' if they felt no information
on the value is present in the text. We thus gather 10 likert-scale
responses per item (\texttt{lyric} / \texttt{speech} excerpt) being
rated, one for each of the 10 Schwartz values, within a range of -1 to
7, and with possible \texttt{NA}s, which comprise the annotations from
each participant.

\paragraph{Comprehension Quiz}\label{comprehension-quiz}

The comprehension quiz asked three questions shown in random order, each
of which comprised of a brief scenario: (1) ``If you feel a SPEAKER
seeks POWER above all else as a life-guiding principle, how would you
respond?'', to which the correct response was at the high end of the
scale (i.e.~5, 6, or 7), (2) ``If you feel a SPEAKER doesn't give any
indication of how important POWER is to them in the lyrics, how would
you respond?'' to which a correct response was ``NA'', and (3) ``If you
feel a SPEAKER avoids POWER more than anything else, or sees it as
completely unimportant to them, how would you respond?'' to which a
correct response was on the lower end of the scale (i.e.~-1, 0, or 1).
Questions were accompanied by the single item, POWER. After participants
responded to each question, the survey displayed the correct response,
along with a brief explanation e.g.~``If you feel a SPEAKER seeks POWER
above all else as a life-guiding principle, respond with a `7',
indicating it is of Supreme Importance to them''.

\paragraph{Attention Check}\label{attention-check}

Our survey checked if participants were paying attention. As per
Prolific guidelines, we include an attention check at the halfway point
of the annotation component of the survey. The check appeared to be
another song lyric, with the lyrics being instructions to participants
to indicate ``7'' for the value of Stimulation, ``0'' for all other
values. Participants who responded with ``-1'' or ``NA'' instead were
also considered passing if they were consistent in the ratings of all
values other than Stimulation. Participants who failed the attention
check were returned to Prolific, and automatically replaced.

\paragraph{Exploratory Measures}\label{exploratory-measures}

We collected a number of exploratory measures which we felt might affect
participant ratings, but for which we had no specific prediction. For
lyrics we asked participants ``Do you think the SPEAKER and the AUTHOR
are the same person?'', and ``Do you recognize the song that the lyrics
come from?'', and for speeches we asked participants ``Do you recognize
the president that delivered the speech?'', and ``Which party do you
think the president belongs to?''.

Following the SSVS for each lyric or speech excerpt, we also assessed
participant confidence in their ratings by asking ``How confident are
you in your ratings of these lyrics? / this speech?'', to which
participants could respond on a scale from 1 (Completely Confident) to 5
(Completely Unconfident). {[}citation about Confidence{]}

For participants completing the survey for Lyrics texts, we also asked a
series of descriptive questions regarding the importance of lyrics in
their music preferences. {[}cite Appendix with exploratory analyses{]}

\subsection{Pilot Methods}\label{pilot-methods}

\subsubsection{Ratings per stimulus}\label{ratings-per-stimulus}

The primary aim of our pilot studies was to estimate the number of
ratings needed per stimulus, to estimate an average perception of a
given value in a given text. We made a determination using boostrapped
estimates of inter-rater reliability for a range of incremental
subsample sizes, as well as correlations between overall sample means
and incremental subsample means. To do so, we followed a procedure
similar to DeBruine \& Jones (2018). This process essentially involves
gathering data comprised of a) a relatively large number of ratings
(e.g.~ratings from 100 raters), on b) a relatively small sub-sample of
the items to be rated (e.g.~15). Inter-rater reliability is repeatedly
estimated on a range of different possible numbers of ratings. Doing so
shows a sampling distribution of inter-rater reliability estimates for
e.g.~5 vs.~10 vs.~15 etc. ratings per stimulus. This essentially allows
for an estimate of a target number of ratings, in order to achieve a
target inter-rater reliability, on average. We estimated the target
number of ratings separately per text type (\texttt{lyrics} /
\texttt{speeches}) as we expected that each form of text could vary in
terms of degree of ambiguity, and separately per value, as we expected
that this degree of ambiguity may vary by value and by text.

As we aim to represent the mean of a population of raters, we estimate
inter-rater reliability as `general consensus' (as opposed to `absolute
agreement') via intraclass correlation coefficient ICC(2,k)(Koo \& Li,
2016). The `2' indicates two-way random effects, in that both the
targets of the ratings and the raters are treated as random samples from
a larger population, and the `k' indicates the reliabiliity of the
average of \emph{k} raters. In essence, ICC(2,k) estimates how
consistently a group mean rating of k raters reflects the ``true'' score
of each item, i.e.~\emph{inter-rater agreement}. We estimate the
inter-rater agreement separately for each value (i.e.~power, achievement
etc.), for the overall sample of ratings, and for each of the subsample
increments (i.e.~5, 10, or 15 ratings) during bootstrapping. We used a
value of 0.75 as a threshold to indicate sufficent, or ``good'',
agreement (Koo \& Li, 2016). To examine the general stability of mean
ratings of a given value in a given text (value / item), we first
estimate a canonical mean per value / item, and then estimate the
correlation between the canonical mean, and the subsample mean per value
/ item. Although we did not see a recommended threshold for pearson
correlations in (DeBruine \& Jones, 2018), we set a threshold of 0.9 to
indicate sufficient, or ``high'' correlation between sub-sample and
overall sample means.

We thus aimed to bootstrap estimates of inter-rater reliability by
incrementally sub-sampling numbers of raters, 50 times per increment,
and then estimating a) inter-rater reliability in the form of consensus
by estimating ICC(2,k), and b) the Pearson correlation between the
sub-sample increment mean and the overall sample mean (DeBruine \&
Jones, 2018). We set the sub-sample increments to 5, ranging from 5 to
50, with replacement, 50 times for each increment: i.e.~we sampled 5
raters 50 times, 10 raters 50 times, 15 raters 50 times etc. up to 50
raters 50 times, all with replacement. We then aim to determine a target
number of ratings based on the distributions of ICC(2,k) and pearson's
r, relative to the thresholds we set. A secondary aim was to pilot our
quiz, and to observe which question(s) participants found to be the most
challenging.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

We tentatively conclude that Ethnicity will have a substantially smaller
effect on ratings of song lyrics than either Ethnicity or Political
Spectrum will have on ratings of speeches. Thus, the sample for our main
study data collection will be stratified by Ethnicity, and for political
speeches, it will also be stratified by political affiliation. We thus
proceed with a target median 15 ratings per song lyric excerpt. Based on
bootstrapped correlations between subsamples and the overall sample, as
well as budgetary constraints, we aim for median 40 ratings per
political speech excerpt. We also observe that some percentage of
participants appear to confuse \texttt{NA} with the low end of the
unbalanced likert scale (-1 or 0).

\section{Appendix A: Stimuli}\label{appendix-a-stimuli}

\subsection{Lyrics Stimuli}\label{lyrics-stimuli}

Songs were sampled across four ``fuzzy'' strata: (1) Year of release,
based on Musixmatch metadata; (2) Popularity, defined as the frequency
with which an artist appeared on playlists in the Million Playlist
Dataset (MPD); (3) Genre, estimated by topic modeling user-generated
artist tags, which were originally drawn from Last.fm and merged into
the MPD; and (4) Lyric topic, derived from topic modeling a bag-of-words
representation of the lyrics. After selecting the final sample of 2,200
songs, Musixmatch provided the first 30\% of the lyrics for each track
via their API.

The code Demetriou et al. (2024) used to sample lyrics can be found
here:
https://anonymous.4open.science/r/lyrics-value-estimators-CE33/1\_stimulus\_sampling/stratified\_sampling.py.

Note: neither the raw lyric data nor the unanonymized repository can be
made available due to limitations in licensing.

\subsubsection{Genre}\label{genre}

Demetriou et al. (2024) used topic modelling applied to a bag-of-words
representation of artist-tag data to estimate one of \(25\) estimated
genres for each included artist, which were then applied to songs. To
get the initial pool, Demetriou et al. (2024) first uniformly sampled
60k artists from the Spotify Million Playlist Dataset (MPD)(Chen et al.,
2018), which contains metadata on \textasciitilde2 million tracks by
\textasciitilde300k artists. They then retrieved the full discographies
of these artists via the Spotify API and cross-checked availability in
Musixmatch, yielding a pool of \textasciitilde510k songs from
\textasciitilde15.4k' artists. We used Latent Dirichlet allocation to
create a topic model of the artist tags in order to derive estimated
genres, shown in the ``Top LDA terms of initial pool'' column of Table
3a.

From this pool, 2,200 songs were selected, and manually screened. As
there is no definitive label for each estimated genre, we derived a
second set of labels aimed at describing the 1,425 artists in the sample
pool, as opposed to the initial 15.4k artist pool. Specifically, we
gathered each unique artist in the 2,200 song sample, and drew the genre
label tags for those artists from using the Spotify and Last.fm APIs. We
then pooled all the artist genre tags for each artist, and estimated the
tf-idf for each tag per genre bin. The top tf-idf terms are shown in the
``Top tf-df terms of sample'' column of table 3a.

\subsubsection{Skew Correction}\label{skew-correction}

Demetriou et al. (2024) observed a skew in data concentration such that
songs that were recent and artists that appeared very frequently in
playlists were most likely be sampled. To compensate, Demetriou et al.
(2024) oversampled less populated bins by increasing the probability
that songs in underpopulated bins are selected, using a
maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) estimate of the categorical distribution of
each stratum (Bassett \& Deride, 2019). Oversampling is controlled via a
concentration parameter \(a\) of the symmetric Dirichlet distribution
which was heuristically set to 40,000, with the aim of between 5\% and
10\% of the overall pool of 2200 songs being drawn from underpopulated
bins.

\subsubsection{Mislabelled `Kept' Songs}\label{mislabelled-kept-songs}

We observe 19 songs in the 380 songs used by Demetriou et al. (2024),
whereby 19 songs are labelled ``remove'' but also ``wave\_1'', which
suggests that those 19 songs were either included erroneously, or
mislabeled ``remove'' erroneously. We elected not to use those 19 songs.

\subsection{Political Speech Stimuli}\label{political-speech-stimuli}

We sampled U.S. State of the Union addresses (post-1900) from the UVA
Miller Center archive. Speeches were sampled across two strata: (1)
President and (2) speech, both of which were labelled by the Miller
center. Speeches were stratified by president and by individual address
so that each president contributed a comparable number of excerpts.
Presidents with fewer addresses contributed more chunks per speech,
while those with many addresses contributed fewer. Each chunk yielded
the first \textasciitilde200 words (ending at a sentence boundary),
producing an approximately balanced set of excerpts across presidents
and speeches. The final pool comprised 300 excerpts for the main study
and 13 each for two pilot studies. Details of the average number of
excerpts by party for both the initial pool and sample are shown in
Table 5. Visualization of number of speeches per president are shown in
Figure 5.

\subsection{Debrief}\label{debrief}

\subsubsection{Lyrics}\label{lyrics-1}

``Thanks for completing the survey!

Our research group, and other scientists like us, are interested in
perceived personal values in song lyrics.

By `values', we mean the abstract concepts or beliefs that we feel are
important as life-guide principles. They have been shown to be related
to our behaviors and goals at the most abstract level, above and beyond
any specific situation. According to Shalom Schwartz, there are 10 main
values that all people share, and each person has their own ranking of
these values. In more recent work, these are broken up further into 19
values, but we used a shorter 10-item version.

This study is helping us learn how to gather data from people about the
values that they think song lyrics express. Our questionnaire about
values is based on one that was a part of the European Social Survey,
and later shortened. You responded to it about yourself at the start of
the study. Respondents like yourself then also completed it about what
they thought of the values of the `speaker' of each song lyric. We will
analyze the data to see whether our version of this questionnaire is
also useful for gathering information on song lyrics.

We expect that different people will interpret the values of lyrics
differently. People might have a preference for different genres of
music or prioritize different values in their lives. People with
different backgrounds (e.g.~ethnicity, gender, level of education, age)
or who prioritize different values might also interpret song lyrics
differently. We used various options on the Prolific platform to screen
respondents like yourself, and also asked a number of questions, to
explore whether any of these factors make a difference in the way people
interpret the values of song lyrics.

Knowing our reasons for the study might have changed the way you would
answer the questions in this survey. For this reason, we only explain
the reasons for our study at the end.

Some songs have lyrics that are difficult or impossible to interpret. We
asked about your confidence in your ratings, to see if some songs are
more vague, or ambiguous than others. We also asked about how subjective
you thought the task was overall, to help us estimate how many
participants we might need for future studies.

De-identified data from this study may be part of published academic
work in the future. We will not include personally identifying
information (e.g.~your Prolific ID), but it will include your responses
to questions about the song lyrics, your music preferences,
demographics, and values. We'd like to remind you that you can request
to withdraw your data at any time by emailing a.m.demetriou@tudelft.nl.
Please be aware that as time passess this will become more challenging
especially if work from this study has been published. We will make all
necessary efforts to remove it, but there is always a risk that we will
not recover every instance of it after it has been published.

Thanks again for participating! Please let us know if you have any
feedback for us below. NOTE: Please do not enter any identifying or
sensitive information in the field.''
